Epilepsy awareness

When you cannot speak for yourself, your information can.

One in twenty-six people will develop epilepsy. Most of us would not know what to do if it happened in front of us. Seizure66 exists to change that - and to give people with epilepsy somewhere private to keep the details that matter in the moments they cannot explain them.

The misunderstanding

Most seizures do not look like the ones on television.

When people picture a seizure they picture someone falling and convulsing. That is one kind - a tonic-clonic seizure - and it is not the most common one.

A seizure can be a person standing still, staring past you, not answering. It can be someone plucking at their clothes, repeating a word, or walking somewhere with no idea why. It can be a wave of nausea, a smell that is not there, or a few seconds that are simply missing afterwards.

People having these seizures are regularly assumed to be drunk, rude, or being difficult. Recognising what is actually happening is the single most useful thing a bystander can do.

If it happens in front of you

Stay. Safe. Side.

Three words worth remembering. They cover most of what a bystander needs to do.

Stay

Stay with them and start timing the seizure. Keep calm, and keep onlookers back.

Safe

Move hard or sharp objects away. Cushion their head. Do not restrain them, and never put anything in their mouth.

Side

Once the movements stop, turn them gently onto their side to keep their airway clear. Stay until they are fully alert.

Call an ambulance if the seizure lasts longer than five minutes, if another follows immediately, if the person is injured, is in water, is pregnant, does not regain consciousness, or if it is their first seizure.
